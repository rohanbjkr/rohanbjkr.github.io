## What can we create from vectors?

In the previous lesson, we learned that vectors can be added and multiplied by scalars.

For example, if

$$
\mathbf{v}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
1
\end{bmatrix},
$$

then we can create

$$
2\mathbf{v}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
4\\
2
\end{bmatrix}
$$

or

$$
-3\mathbf{v}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
-6\\
-3
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

We can also combine different vectors.

For example, if

$$
\mathbf{u}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

and

$$
\mathbf{v}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1
\end{bmatrix},
$$

then

$$
2\mathbf{u}+3\mathbf{v}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
3
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

This raises a natural question:

> **If we are allowed to add vectors and multiply them by scalars, what vectors can we create?**

This is the idea behind **span**.

---

## 1. Start with one vector

Let's begin with just one vector:

$$
\mathbf{v}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

We can multiply $\mathbf v$ by any real number $c$.

So we can create

$$
c\mathbf{v}
=
c
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
c\\
2c
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Let's try a few values.

If $c=1$,

$$
\mathbf v=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

If $c=2$,

$$
2\mathbf v=
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
4
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

If $c=-1$,

$$
-\mathbf v=
\begin{bmatrix}
-1\\
-2
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

If $c=0$,

$$
0\mathbf v=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
0
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

We can create infinitely many vectors.

But notice something important.

All of these vectors lie on the **same line**.

The vector points in one direction, and multiplying by a scalar only changes its length and possibly reverses its direction.

So one vector can generate an entire line through the origin.

---

## 2. The line generated by one vector

Consider again

$$
\mathbf v=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Every vector we can create has the form

$$
c\mathbf v.
$$

Therefore,

$$
c\mathbf v
=
\begin{bmatrix}
c\\
2c
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

If we call the components $x$ and $y$, then

$$
x=c,
\qquad
y=2c.
$$

Therefore,

$$
y=2x.
$$

This is the equation of a line through the origin.

So we have discovered something important:

> **The multiples of one nonzero vector form a line through the origin.**

We write this collection of vectors as

$$
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf v\}.
$$

For our example,

$$
\operatorname{span}
\left\{
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2
\end{bmatrix}
\right\}
$$

is the line

$$
y=2x.
$$

---

## 3. What does "span" mean?

The word **span** has a simple intuition.

It means:

> **Everything we can reach using the vectors we have, by scaling them and adding them.**

For one vector $\mathbf v$,

$$
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf v\}
$$

means all possible multiples of $\mathbf v$:

$$
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf v\}
=
\{c\mathbf v:c\in\mathbb R\}.
$$

For example,

$$
\operatorname{span}
\left\{
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2
\end{bmatrix}
\right\}
=
\left\{
c
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2
\end{bmatrix}
:
c\in\mathbb R
\right\}.
$$

That is simply the line through the origin in the direction of $\mathbf v$.

---

## 4. Now add a second vector

One vector gives us only one direction.

What happens if we have two vectors?

Consider

$$
\mathbf u=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

and

$$
\mathbf v=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Now we can create expressions of the form

$$
c_1\mathbf u+c_2\mathbf v.
$$

For example,

$$
2\mathbf u+3\mathbf v
=
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
3
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Or

$$
-1\mathbf u+4\mathbf v
=
\begin{bmatrix}
-1\\
4
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Or

$$
5\mathbf u-2\mathbf v
=
\begin{bmatrix}
5\\
-2
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

What vectors can we create?

Let's calculate the general expression.

---

## 5. All linear combinations of the two vectors

We have

$$
c_1\mathbf u+c_2\mathbf v.
$$

Substituting the vectors,

$$
c_1
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0
\end{bmatrix}
+
c_2
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
=
\begin{bmatrix}
c_1\\
c_2
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

But $c_1$ and $c_2$ can be **any real numbers**.

Therefore, we can create any vector

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
x\\
y
\end{bmatrix}
\in\mathbb R^2.
$$

So these two vectors span the entire plane.

We write

$$
\operatorname{span}
\left\{
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0
\end{bmatrix},
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1
\end{bmatrix}
\right\}
=
\mathbb R^2.
$$

This is a much larger span than the span of one vector.

---

## 6. The geometry of span

We can now see a pattern.

###### One nonzero vector

Its multiples form a line:

$$
\text{vector}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\text{line}.
$$

###### Two independent vectors in $\mathbb R^2$

Their linear combinations fill the plane:

$$
\text{two directions}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\text{plane}.
$$

This gives us a useful geometric picture.

The key question is always:

> **How much space can these vectors reach?**

---

## 7. A different pair of vectors

Now consider

$$
\mathbf u=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

and

$$
\mathbf v=
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
2
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

At first glance, we have two vectors.

Perhaps we might expect them to span the plane.

But look carefully:

$$
\mathbf v=2\mathbf u.
$$

The second vector is just a multiple of the first.

So adding the second vector does not give us a new direction.

A general linear combination is

$$
c_1\mathbf u+c_2\mathbf v.
$$

Since

$$
\mathbf v=2\mathbf u,
$$

we get

$$
c_1\mathbf u+c_2(2\mathbf u).
$$

Therefore,

$$
=
(c_1+2c_2)\mathbf u.
$$

This is still just a multiple of $\mathbf u$.

So the span is still only a line.

We have

$$
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u,\mathbf v\}
=
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u\}.
$$

Adding a vector that points in an existing direction does not enlarge the span.

---

## 8. This is an important distinction

Compare the two examples.

###### Example A

$$
\mathbf u=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf v=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

These vectors point in different directions.

Together they span

$$
\mathbb R^2.
$$

###### Example B

$$
\mathbf u=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
1
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf v=
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
2
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

These vectors point in the same direction.

Together they span only a line.

So the number of vectors is not enough.

What matters is whether the vectors provide **new directions**.

This idea will eventually lead us to **linear independence**.

---

## 9. Formal definition of span

We can now give the formal definition.

Suppose we have vectors

$$
\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2,\ldots,\mathbf v_k.
$$

Their span is the set of **all linear combinations** of these vectors:

$$
\boxed{
\operatorname{span}
\{\mathbf v_1,\ldots,\mathbf v_k\}
=
\left\{
c_1\mathbf v_1+\cdots+c_k\mathbf v_k
:
c_1,\ldots,c_k\in\mathbb R
\right\}.
}
$$

In words:

> The span of a collection of vectors is everything that can be produced by scaling those vectors and adding the results.

This definition is worth understanding rather than memorizing.

---

## 10. Span of one vector

For one vector,

$$
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf v\}
=
\{c\mathbf v:c\in\mathbb R\}.
$$

If $\mathbf v\neq\mathbf0$, this is a line through the origin.

For example,

$$
\operatorname{span}
\left\{
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
1
\end{bmatrix}
\right\}
$$

is the line

$$
y=\frac12x.
$$

---

## 11. Span of two vectors in $\mathbb R^2$

Suppose

$$
\mathbf u=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

and

$$
\mathbf v=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Then

$$
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u,\mathbf v\}
=
\mathbb R^2.
$$

Why?

Because every vector

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
x\\
y
\end{bmatrix}
$$

can be written as

$$
x
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0
\end{bmatrix}
+
y
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
x\\
y
\end{bmatrix}
=
x\mathbf u+y\mathbf v.
$$

Since $x$ and $y$ can be any real numbers, every vector in $\mathbb R^2$ is reachable.

---

## 12. Can two vectors always span $\mathbb R^2$?

No.

Consider

$$
\mathbf u=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2
\end{bmatrix}
$$

and

$$
\mathbf v=
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
4
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Since

$$
\mathbf v=2\mathbf u,
$$

both vectors point in the same direction.

A general combination is

$$
c_1\mathbf u+c_2\mathbf v
=
(c_1+2c_2)\mathbf u.
$$

So every vector we create is still on the same line.

Therefore,

$$
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u,\mathbf v\}
$$

is a line, not the entire plane.

---

## 13. How can we tell whether two vectors span the plane?

For vectors in $\mathbb R^2$, the key question is:

> **Are they pointing in genuinely different directions?**

Suppose

$$
\mathbf u=
\begin{bmatrix}
u_1\\
u_2
\end{bmatrix}
$$

and

$$
\mathbf v=
\begin{bmatrix}
v_1\\
v_2
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

If one is a scalar multiple of the other, they span only a line.

If neither is a scalar multiple of the other, they point in different directions, and together they span $\mathbb R^2$.

For example,

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0
\end{bmatrix}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

span the plane.

But

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2
\end{bmatrix}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
4
\end{bmatrix}
$$

do not.

---

## 14. Span in $\mathbb R^3$

Now let's move from the plane to three-dimensional space.

Consider the three vectors

$$
\mathbf e_1=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0\\
0
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf e_2=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1\\
0
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf e_3=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
0\\
1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Take any vector in $\mathbb R^3$:

$$
\mathbf x=
\begin{bmatrix}
x\\
y\\
z
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

We can write

$$
\mathbf x
=
x\mathbf e_1
+
y\mathbf e_2
+
z\mathbf e_3.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2,\mathbf e_3\}
=
\mathbb R^3.
$$

The three standard directions allow us to reach every point in three-dimensional space.

---

## 15. What if we only have two vectors in $\mathbb R^3$?

Consider

$$
\mathbf u=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0\\
0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

and

$$
\mathbf v=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1\\
0
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

A general linear combination is

$$
c_1\mathbf u+c_2\mathbf v.
$$

Therefore,

$$
c_1
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0\\
0
\end{bmatrix}
+
c_2
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1\\
0
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
c_1\\
c_2\\
0
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Notice the third component.

It is always zero.

So we cannot reach vectors such as

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2\\
3
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The span is the $xy$-plane:

$$
z=0.
$$

Thus,

$$
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u,\mathbf v\}
$$

is a plane inside $\mathbb R^3$, not all of $\mathbb R^3$.

---

## 16. The dimension of the span

We can now see a geometric pattern.

In $\mathbb R^2$:

- one nonzero direction gives a line;
- two independent directions give a plane.

In $\mathbb R^3$:

- one nonzero direction gives a line;
- two independent directions give a plane;
- three independent directions can give all of $\mathbb R^3$.

This suggests that the number of genuinely different directions matters.

We will eventually formalize this idea using **dimension**.

For now, the important point is:

> **The span describes the space of all possible directions and combinations generated by our vectors.**

---

## 17. Span and matrix equations

Now let's return to something we already know:

$$
A\mathbf x=\mathbf b.
$$

Suppose the columns of $A$ are

$$
A=
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf a_1&
\mathbf a_2&
\cdots&
\mathbf a_n
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Then

$$
A\mathbf x
=
x_1\mathbf a_1
+x_2\mathbf a_2
+\cdots
+x_n\mathbf a_n.
$$

But this expression is a linear combination of the columns of $A$.

Therefore,

$$
A\mathbf x=\mathbf b
$$

asks:

> **Can $\mathbf b$ be written as a linear combination of the columns of $A$?**

In other words:

> **Is $\mathbf b$ in the span of the columns of $A$?**

This is one of the most important connections in linear algebra.

---

## 18. A simple example

Consider

$$
A=
\begin{bmatrix}
1&0\\
0&1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Its columns are

$$
\mathbf a_1=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf a_2=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

We already know that these two vectors span $\mathbb R^2$.

Therefore, for **every**

$$
\mathbf b\in\mathbb R^2,
$$

there is some $\mathbf x$ such that

$$
A\mathbf x=\mathbf b.
$$

For example, take

$$
\mathbf b=
\begin{bmatrix}
4\\
7
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

We can write

$$
\mathbf b
=
4
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0
\end{bmatrix}
+
7
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

So the system has a solution.

---

## 19. An example where the span is only a line

Now consider

$$
A=
\begin{bmatrix}
1&2\\
2&4
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Its columns are

$$
\mathbf a_1=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2
\end{bmatrix}
$$

and

$$
\mathbf a_2=
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
4
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

But

$$
\mathbf a_2=2\mathbf a_1.
$$

Therefore, the columns point in the same direction.

Their span is only the line

$$
y=2x.
$$

Now consider

$$
\mathbf b=
\begin{bmatrix}
3\\
6
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

This vector lies on the line.

Indeed,

$$
\mathbf b
=
3
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

So $\mathbf b$ is in the span of the columns.

But consider

$$
\mathbf b=
\begin{bmatrix}
3\\
5
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

This vector does not lie on the line $y=2x$.

Therefore it cannot be written as a linear combination of the columns.

So the system

$$
A\mathbf x=\mathbf b
$$

has no solution for this $\mathbf b$.

---

## 20. Span answers a question we already had

Earlier, when studying

$$
A\mathbf x=\mathbf b,
$$

we asked:

> When does a system have a solution?

Now we can give a geometric answer.

The system

$$
A\mathbf x=\mathbf b
$$

has a solution **if and only if**

$$
\mathbf b
$$

belongs to the span of the columns of $A$.

In symbols,

$$
\boxed{
A\mathbf x=\mathbf b
\text{ has a solution}
\iff
\mathbf b\in\operatorname{span}\{\text{columns of }A\}.
}
$$

This is a major step in understanding linear algebra.

---

## 21. Span is about reachability

A useful way to think about span is:

> **Span tells us what we can reach.**

Suppose our available vectors are

$$
\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2,\ldots,\mathbf v_k.
$$

We can scale them:

$$
c_1\mathbf v_1,\ldots,c_k\mathbf v_k.
$$

Then we can add them:

$$
c_1\mathbf v_1+\cdots+c_k\mathbf v_k.
$$

The collection of everything we can reach is

$$
\operatorname{span}
\{\mathbf v_1,\ldots,\mathbf v_k\}.
$$

So span is fundamentally a question about **possibility**.

What vectors can these vectors generate?

---

## 22. One more important example

Consider

$$
\mathbf u=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
1
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf v=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
-1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Do these two vectors span $\mathbb R^2$?

Let's try to create an arbitrary vector

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
x\\
y
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

We want

$$
c_1
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
1
\end{bmatrix}
+
c_2
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
-1
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
x\\
y
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Multiplying out,

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
c_1+c_2\\
c_1-c_2
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
x\\
y
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Therefore we need

$$
c_1+c_2=x
$$

and

$$
c_1-c_2=y.
$$

Adding these equations:

$$
2c_1=x+y.
$$

Therefore,

$$
c_1=\frac{x+y}{2}.
$$

Similarly,

$$
c_2=\frac{x-y}{2}.
$$

These coefficients exist for **every** $x$ and $y$.

Therefore,

$$
\operatorname{span}
\left\{
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
1
\end{bmatrix},
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
-1
\end{bmatrix}
\right\}
=
\mathbb R^2.
$$

The vectors do not have to be the standard basis vectors.

They simply need to provide enough independent directions.

---

## 23. What have we learned?

We started with a simple question:

> **What can we create from vectors?**

With one vector,

$$
c\mathbf v
$$

gives all multiples of that vector.

With two vectors,

$$
c_1\mathbf u+c_2\mathbf v
$$

gives all their possible linear combinations.

With several vectors,

$$
c_1\mathbf v_1+\cdots+c_k\mathbf v_k
$$

gives all possible linear combinations of those vectors.

The set of all these combinations is their **span**.

Formally,

$$
\operatorname{span}
\{\mathbf v_1,\ldots,\mathbf v_k\}
=
\left\{
c_1\mathbf v_1+\cdots+c_k\mathbf v_k
:
c_i\in\mathbb R
\right\}.
$$

And when we return to

$$
A\mathbf x=\mathbf b,
$$

we now understand that the question is:

> **Is $\mathbf b$ in the span of the columns of $A$?**

---

## Try It Yourself

Consider

$$
\mathbf u=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf v=
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
4
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

###### Question 1

Can $\mathbf v$ provide a new direction beyond $\mathbf u$?

###### Question 2

What is

$$
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u,\mathbf v\}?
$$

###### Question 3

Can the vector

$$
\mathbf b=
\begin{bmatrix}
3\\
6
\end{bmatrix}
$$

be created from $\mathbf u$ and $\mathbf v$?

###### Question 4

Can the vector

$$
\mathbf c=
\begin{bmatrix}
3\\
5
\end{bmatrix}
$$

be created from $\mathbf u$ and $\mathbf v$?

---

## Answers

###### Answer 1

Yes, we can check whether $\mathbf v$ is a multiple of $\mathbf u$:

$$
\mathbf v
=
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
4
\end{bmatrix}
=
2
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\mathbf v=2\mathbf u.
$$

It does not provide a new direction.

---

###### Answer 2

Since $\mathbf v$ is already a multiple of $\mathbf u$,

$$
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u,\mathbf v\}
=
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u\}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u,\mathbf v\}
=
\left\{
c
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2
\end{bmatrix}
:c\in\mathbb R
\right\}.
$$

Geometrically, this is the line

$$
y=2x.
$$

---

###### Answer 3

We want to determine whether

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
3\\
6
\end{bmatrix}
$$

lies on the line $y=2x$.

It does, because

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
3\\
6
\end{bmatrix}
=
3
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
\mathbf b\in
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u,\mathbf v\}.
}
$$

---

###### Answer 4

Consider

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
3\\
5
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

If it were in the span, it would have to be of the form

$$
c
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
3\\
5
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The first component would require

$$
c=3.
$$

But then the second component would be

$$
2c=6,
$$

not $5$.

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
\begin{bmatrix}
3\\
5
\end{bmatrix}
\notin
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u,\mathbf v\}.
}
$$

---

## A final connection

We have now developed an important chain of ideas:

$$
\text{Vectors}
\longrightarrow
\text{Linear combinations}
\longrightarrow
\text{Span}.
$$

And we have learned that span tells us **what vectors can be created**.

But something interesting happened in our examples.

We saw that

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2
\end{bmatrix}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
4
\end{bmatrix}
$$

do not really give us two directions.

The second vector is already contained in the first.

So one of the vectors is redundant.

This leads to the next natural question:

> **When are a collection of vectors genuinely independent?**

That is the idea of **linear independence**.

---

## What is next?

Next we will study:

$$
\boxed{\text{Linear Independence}}
$$

We will ask:

> Can one of our vectors be created from the others?

If the answer is yes, the vectors contain redundancy.
